Key points

  • Aggregates size a problem and retained traces explain it: take population rates from full-population counters, not an oversampled trace store.
  • Head sampling cannot ensure every error is kept: a 10% head stage delivers about 10% of errors to the tail policy.
  • Tail sampling needs every span of a trace on one collector instance; size decision_wait and buffers from measured arrival rates.
  • Keep exact tenant, session, and trace identifiers out of metric labels: an overflowed stream's total stays correct while grouped queries undercount.
  • A task outcome is the final state in the environment, not what the final message claims; tool status is a separate measurement.

Trace analysis at scale uses many traces and population measurements to identify and quantify patterns across attempts. A single trace shows one timeout, not a timeout rate that tripled after a prompt deploy. The skill combines sampling (which traces you keep), aggregation (what the population did), and failure-cohort analysis (what differs between groups of attempts).

How it works

Two stores feed one comparison. Full-population aggregates are counters and histograms computed across the intended population before trace sampling; their denominators are complete only when instrumentation and delivery are complete. Retained traces are the spans and events a sampling policy kept, so they carry the mechanism. Aggregates size a problem; traces explain it.

Head sampling decides as early as possible, and that decision is not made by inspecting the trace as a whole. Tail sampling decides by considering all or most of the spans in the trace, so it can apply criteria that appear late. That gap is why tail sampling exists: with head sampling alone you cannot ensure that all traces containing an error are sampled. Some systems combine head and tail sampling. When head sampling comes first, a 10% uniform head sampler independent of failure, feeding a policy that keeps every received error, delivers in expectation roughly 10% of errors; the tail policy cannot recover what the head stage dropped.

Strategy Decision point Coverage you can promise Reach for it when
Head Early, without the full trace. No guarantee of error coverage. Volume must fall before any state is built
Tail After all or most spans. Trace-wide properties, if spans arrive intact and in time. Failures, slow traces, and rare paths need explanation
Head then tail Head first, tail second. Only the errors that survived the head stage Full volume will not fit in tail-sampling state

Sampling design and collector capacity

The OTel Collector's tail_sampling processor keeps spans in memory while it waits to decide, and all spans for a given trace must be sent to the same Collector instance. Its configuration requires policies. The status_code policy samples on OK, ERROR, or UNSET. A latency policy measures earliest span start to latest span end, not one tool's execution. decision_wait is the time before timer handling for a trace and defaults to 30s; num_traces bounds buffered traces.

Capacity pressure can discard traces before sampling. By default the collector evicts old traces when num_traces is reached; block_on_overflow can instead wait for space. Spans arriving after the decision can cause different sampling decisions for different parts of the same trace. Monitor otelcol_processor_tail_sampling_sampling_trace_dropped_too_early, otelcol_processor_tail_sampling_sampling_trace_removal_age, and otelcol_processor_tail_sampling_sampling_late_span_age, route by trace ID to the sampling tier, and size decision_wait and buffers to the observed workload. The first metric warns of telemetry loss, not application failure. Treat any "100% of failures" claim as conditional: the trace reached this collector, the decision came after the failure appeared, and the buffer had room.

Long-running agents sharpen that trade-off: estimate concurrent traces as arrival rate times decision wait, add burst headroom, and validate against live metrics. A short wait can decide before the task fails; a long wait grows retained state, so split workflows that outlive the window with explicit task correlation.

Design the policy in layers: reserve a known-probability baseline across all traffic, then oversample errors, slow traces, new routes and models, small tenants, and rare tool paths. Give known low-volume strata a higher documented rate, inside your privacy and volume limits, and keep the sampling rate, policy version, and loss counters with every observation. A rare route first visible after sampling needs an upstream signal or a policy redesign.

Aggregation, denominators, and cardinality

Define the statistical unit before you aggregate: one completed user task or attempt, with a stable task ID and one independently assessed terminal outcome. Track incomplete, timed-out, and ungraded attempts separately, because silently excluding them makes failure rates look better. Join spans and events to the attempt without counting retries, tool calls, and model calls as separate tasks, and keep technical error separate from an unmet user objective.

Prefer full-population counters or pre-sampling aggregates for task starts, completed outcomes, and tool latency histograms; a metric denominator beats a trace-store count. A store holding 100% of errors plus 5% of successes is excellent for investigation and terrible as a rate: its raw failure fraction is biased upward. Where per-trace inclusion probabilities are known and nonzero, estimate counts by inverse inclusion probability and report weighted failures over weighted attempts. Overlapping policies need the union inclusion probability, not the sum of policy percentages, and 1/p is wrong for a rate-limited or adaptive policy whose realized probability is unknown. Quantify uncertainty on sampled estimates.

Cardinality is the number of distinct attribute combinations in a metric stream. The OpenTelemetry metrics SDK aggregates measurements by the full set of attribute values, and each unique attribute combination becomes a separate data point tracked in memory. When a metric stream overflows, the total value remains correct while queries that filter or group by attributes can undercount. So exact tenant, task, user, session, prompt, and trace identifiers belong in restricted trace search, not in metric labels: a group suppressed by overflow can look failure-free while its counts vanish. Keep bounded route templates, model families, prompt versions, and approved tenant classes on dashboards, and keep granular cohort analysis in a governed store with overflow monitored and small public cohorts suppressed.

Aggregation also finds patterns no individual record reveals. Clio (renamed Anthropic Insights in the source's 2026 update) does bottom-up discovery by distilling conversations into abstracted topic clusters, keeps a minimum threshold on unique users or conversations so low-frequency topics are not inadvertently exposed, and has identified coordinated misuse invisible when looking at individual conversations. Treat it as an analogy for cohort-level discovery and small-group suppression, not a trace product or a turnkey collector: these safeguards are not a formal privacy guarantee merely because they resemble its design.

Failure-cohort analysis

Failure-cohort analysis compares outcomes between groups of attempts to find and quantify systemic patterns; comparison alone does not establish a cause. Build one task-level table with time window, deployment, requested and actual model, application prompt version, tenant or approved tenant class, route template, tool or server, outcome, latency, and sample probability. Group by one dimension first, then targeted combinations, and compare counts and failure rates, each cohort's share of total failures, latency p50 and p95, and a baseline. Relative risk and total impact differ: a cohort can be 5x worse and still be a rounding error, while a small increase on a high-volume route dominates the incident. Check mix shifts, including Simpson's paradox, with standardized or matched comparisons.

For tool attribution, compare P(task failure | tool latency bucket, route, model, prompt, tenant class) against tool error and timeout rates, task latency, and retry counts. Align the call that precedes the terminal failure, account for multiple calls per task, and pick one per-task statistic such as maximum or first relevant call. A slow tool can be a consequence of task difficulty, workload, or a retry storm rather than the cause, so causality needs rollout timing, a controlled intervention, or randomization.

Transcript review and next measurement

Inspect representative transcripts from failing and successful cohorts, including tool arguments and results where policy permits. Check whether the agent adapted, whether the tool returned a meaningful result, whether a timeout or permission rejection occurred, and whether grading matched the external outcome. Convert confirmed patterns into held-out regression evals; do not let the same samples define and prove the fix.

The next measurement is a controlled comparison. During a v1/v2 split inside tenant A, record one row per attempt with assignment, task type, OAuth refresh and 401 and retry timing, lookup latency, deadline, timeout reason, and externally verified outcome. Compare failure and latency rates by assigned version and task type with counts and confidence intervals, and read sampled transcripts, including T5-like recoveries. Give both variants similar traffic and account for in-flight tasks and data loss. Without randomization, compare matched windows and workload mix, and label the result observational.

Decision criteria

  1. Estimating a population rate? Use complete counters or a known-probability baseline sample, not the raw composition of an oversampled trace store.
  2. Explaining a failure invisible in aggregate? Tail-sample on trace-wide properties such as error status.
  3. Protecting a rare path? Raise its documented rate upstream; a head stage before the tail policy caps what any downstream rule can retain.
  4. Seeing trace loss or split traces? Fix trace-ID-affine routing, decision_wait, and buffer capacity before concluding anything about the application.
  5. A global failure rate moved? Rebuild task-based denominators, track incomplete attempts, then segment by model, prompt version, tenant class, route, and tool.
  6. Exposing raw transcripts or tenant IDs? Keep metric labels bounded, guard against overflow undercounting, and route detail review through a governed store.

Before committing, also report the window, the probability actually applied, collector loss counters, the boundaries behind each latency number, and grader validation against environment outcomes.

Failure modes and recovery

Common mistake Reading the failure rate off the trace store

An oversampled store's failure fraction is a property of your policy, not of production: a 100%-error rule plus a 5%-success rule manufactures a plausible rate out of thin air. Head-dropped traces are unrecoverable downstream, since the tail decision cannot inspect spans that never arrived. Take the rate from full-population counters, and report sampled rates only with known inclusion probabilities and an uncertainty range.

Common mistake Treating a tool ERROR as a task failure

Tool status and task outcome are different measurements. claude_code.tool_result is absent for rejected tool calls, and on the API side tool_error can indicate a tool that raised or returned an error result. A retry can absorb a tool error, as T5 illustrates. A task outcome is the final state in the environment, so define the task unit and keep technical error separate from an unmet user objective.

Common mistake Claiming full failure coverage while the collector drops traces

The tail processor holds spans in memory and needs every span of a trace on one instance. Capacity pressure can drop traces before sampling, and late spans can lead to different decisions. Recover by routing on trace ID, sizing decision_wait and buffers from measured arrival rates, and publishing the drop and late-span metrics beside the coverage claim.

The last trap sits in the numbers, not the pipeline: latency comparisons can mislead when boundaries differ, since the execution span measures only the tool body while the encompassing span may include permission wait. A healthy dashboard total beside a shrinking filtered view can indicate metric overflow; verify overflow before re-deriving the cohort from the governed store.

Exam lens

Exam lenseditorial inference

  • Many healthy traces, rare costly failures: choose explicit rare and failure coverage plus a known-probability baseline over uniform-only sampling.
  • Trace loss or split traces: fix trace-affine routing, buffer and wait tuning, and collector health before concluding the application improved.
  • A changed global failure rate: rebuild task-based denominators, track incomplete attempts, and segment by model, prompt, tenant, route, and tool before causal attribution.
  • High-cardinality identifiers and sensitive transcripts: bounded metrics, governed detail review, and a privacy-aware sampling policy, with small-group suppression designed apart from rare-path capture.

Self-check

QA 10% head sampler feeds a tail policy that keeps all errors. What fraction of errors reaches the tail policy?Show answer
A

In expectation about 10%, assuming independent uniform head sampling. The tail policy decides over traces it actually received, and head sampling alone cannot ensure every trace containing an error is sampled, so the rest are gone.

QSampled traces show 100 failures and 100 successes, with known inclusion probabilities of 1 and 0.1. What is the estimated failure rate?Show answer
A

100 / (100 + 100 / 0.1) = about 9.1%. Quantify its uncertainty, and remember that further unaccounted-for drops can bias it.

QWhy inspect both successful and failed slow-tool traces?Show answer
A

To see whether latency preceded the failure, how a successful run recovered, and whether the apparent pattern or the grader is misleading. Read transcripts and grades across many trials to check graders, and a plausible final answer is not the outcome — the outcome is the final state in the environment.
