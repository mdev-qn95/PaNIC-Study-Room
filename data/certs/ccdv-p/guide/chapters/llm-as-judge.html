Key points

  • Calibrate the judge against human labels on a held-out slice before it earns authority to block a release.
  • Call the judge twice with the answers swapped: declare a win only when an answer is preferred in both orders, score flips as ties.
  • Two complete raters: Cohen's kappa. Three or more raters, missing overlap, or ordinal scales: Krippendorff's alpha.
  • A pre-4.6 dateless ID such as claude-sonnet-4-5 is a moving alias; pin the snapshot and re-score a frozen anchor set.
  • Asking the judge to print its reasoning can trigger a reasoning_extraction refusal, which has no recommended fallback model.

An LLM-as-judge is a model that grades another model's output against a rubric, standing in for human review at evaluation scale. Anthropic recommends careful iteration and close calibration against human experts until human and model scores show little divergence. Once a grading system is robust, Anthropic says occasional human review is sufficient. The decision this chapter supports is narrow: does this judge deserve authority to block a release?

Choosing the grading method

Anthropic compares code-based checks, LLM-based grading, and human grading by speed, reliability, and scalability, and advises choosing the fastest, most reliable, most scalable method that fits the judgment.

Grader Strength Weakness Use it for
Code-based Fastest and most reliable, extremely scalable Lacks nuance for complex judgments that need less rule-based rigidity Schema checks, required fields, exact values
LLM-based Fast, flexible, scalable, suitable for complex judgment Reliability is unproven until you test it Groundedness, policy compliance, synthesis, preference
Human Most flexible and high quality Slow and expensive; Anthropic advises avoiding it if possible Judge calibration and subjective outputs, where human consensus is the reference standard

Grading recurs on every evaluation run, which makes per-run grader speed and price a central design constraint. Anthropic accordingly conditions LLM grading on testing for reliability before scaling, and on trying the grader against real samples to see whether the task suits it.

Rubric design

A rubric is the explicit criteria list the judge applies to each item. Anthropic recommends detailed, clear rubrics and notes that one use case or criterion may need several rubrics for holistic evaluation. Make the rubric observable: replace subjective adjectives with conditions a reviewer can check. Two domain experts should independently reach the same pass/fail verdict on a good task, because ambiguity in task specifications becomes noise in metrics and vague grader criteria produce inconsistent judgments.

Three mechanics carry most of the accuracy:

  • Empirical outputs. Instruct the judge to output only correct or incorrect, or to judge on a scale of 1–5; purely qualitative evaluations are hard to assess quickly and at scale.
  • Isolated dimensions. Write a structured rubric per dimension and grade each with an isolated judge rather than one judge for all dimensions, so a strong aspect cannot mask a failing one; move rule-shaped checks to code graders.
  • An escape hatch. Let the judge return Unknown when it lacks enough information, which avoids hallucinated verdicts on truncated or ambiguous transcripts.

Enable thinking in the grader so it reasons before producing a score, which Anthropic reports improves evaluation performance on tasks requiring complex judgment. Balance the dataset across cases where a behavior should occur and where it should not, because one-sided evals create one-sided optimization.

Grading mode: pointwise, pairwise, and reference-guided

The grading mode sets the judge's cost curve and its sensitivity. Zheng et al. (2023) contrast pairwise comparison, where possible pairs grow quadratically as candidates increase, with single-answer pointwise grading, which may fail to discern subtle differences between specific pairs and whose absolute scores may fluctuate more when the judge model changes.

Mode Cost shape Use it for Main risk
Pointwise Linear in items CI/CD regression gates, SLA tracking, rates compared across builds Absolute scores drift when the judge changes
Pairwise Quadratic in candidates Subtle A/B preference tests, scoped to targeted pairs Cost blowup at scale, plus slot bias (below)
Reference-guided Pointwise plus golden answers Math, reasoning, factual checks Judge falls back on parametric memory without a reference

Compare pointwise scores across builds only when the judge snapshot is pinned, run pairwise judgments on targeted pairs rather than a full matrix, and give reference-guided runs a golden answer.

Bias and position effects

Position bias is an LLM judge's propensity to favor answers in specific prompt positions over others. Verbosity bias makes a judge favor longer, verbose responses even when they are not as clear, high-quality, or accurate as shorter alternatives, and self-enhancement bias is the possibility that a judge favors answers generated by itself. Zheng et al. said their data could not determine whether judges exhibit this bias. In their study, strong judges such as GPT-4 reached over 80% agreement with human preferences on MT-Bench and Chatbot Arena, matching human-human agreement.

The standard mitigation is conservative order swapping: call the judge twice with the answers swapped, declare a win only when an answer is preferred in both orders, and score inconsistent results as a tie. Swapping uses two judge calls per pair; the Message Batches API offers a 50 percent discount on eligible batch usage. For large sweeps, randomly assign which candidate occupies Slot 1; retain bidirectional swaps on a calibration subset to measure flip rate. Counter verbosity bias with an explicit conciseness requirement and length-adversarial cases. Treat self-enhancement as a possible bias to measure against human labels, not an established universal effect.

Consider this illustrative decision pattern, aligned with Official Sample Item 2 in the exam guide: a team compares two answers with an uncalibrated pairwise judge and always puts Candidate B in Slot 1. The initial win rate appears favorable, but the team has not separated position effects from answer quality. It should rerun the same items with answer order swapped and compare judge labels with human labels before deciding whether the judge is reliable enough to gate a release. A swap flip is evidence of position sensitivity; count it as a tie or send it for human review. If disagreement analysis finds unclear criteria, split the rubric into observable dimensions, enable thinking for complex judgments, and allow an Unknown verdict when evidence is insufficient. Treat any subsequent improvement as something to measure on held-out human-labeled items, not an assumed outcome.

Calibrating the judge against human labels

Calibration measures judge agreement with human labels on a held-out slice before the judge touches a pipeline. Run it in this order:

  1. Curate a stratified, balanced set spanning positive, negative or trap, and edge cases.
  2. Have at least two SMEs label it independently and reconcile disagreements; if their agreement is poor, fix the rubric and task spec before tuning the judge.
  3. Run the pinned judge; compute judge-versus-human agreement plus a confusion matrix of false passes and false fails.
  4. Read every disagreement transcript to separate rubric gaps from harness truncation from bias, then re-measure on a held-out slice.

Inter-rater reliability (IRR) closes the loop, and the two standard statistics answer different questions. Cohen's kappa measures chance-corrected agreement between exactly two raters who each classify N items into C mutually exclusive categories, and is restricted to two annotators on single-label ratings. Krippendorff's alpha handles any number of coders, incomplete or missing data, and nominal, ordinal, interval, or ratio scales, so it fits two SMEs plus a judge and 1–5 rubrics, where a 1-versus-5 disagreement should count more than 4-versus-5.

Situation Metric Threshold to act on
Two raters, complete categorical labels (pass/fail, A/B/tie) Cohen's kappa Above 0.8 is good agreement; at or below 0, no better than chance
Three or more raters, missing overlap, or ordinal/interval scales Krippendorff's alpha 0.800 and above is reliable; 0.667 to below 0.800 supports tentative conclusions only
High-stakes automated gating Either The minimum acceptable alpha should be set by the cost of mistaken conclusions

When class distributions differ, kappa can be lower even when raw agreement is the same, because the expected chance agreement differs. Report minority-class precision and recall and inspect a balanced slice alongside kappa.

Judge request mechanics

The judge's request parameters are part of the instrument, so version them with the prompt. For verdict capture, the Anthropic Python SDK accepts a Pydantic model as output_format in client.messages.parse() and returns the parsed model in parsed_output. Structured outputs send output_config.format with type: "json_schema". The constrained-decoding grammar applies only to Claude's direct output, not to thinking blocks, so the judge can think before returning schema-constrained JSON. Structured outputs do not guarantee the capitalization of enum and const values, so normalize labels with .strip().lower() in the parser.

Do not ask the judge to print its reasoning. Prompting Claude to put its thinking or reasoning in the output, verbatim or in a fixed format — legacy <thinking></thinking> tags, or a schema property named reasoning or thinking — can trigger a reasoning_extraction refusal. The refusal response uses stop_reason: "refusal", and this category has no recommended fallback model, so change the prompt rather than retry: inspect summarized thinking blocks and ask the schema for a short explanation plus cited evidence. Thinking display defaults to omitted on Claude 5 and later models, hiding thinking text until you opt in. To trade thoroughness against token efficiency, set effort in output_config; Anthropic describes effort as a way to control token use and response thoroughness.

The following Python illustrates a groundedness grade with an unknown escape hatch, then a pairwise judge with order swapping. It uses the documented Pydantic parse() pattern and pins the Claude Sonnet 4.6 snapshot ID.

from enum import Enum
from typing import Literal
from pydantic import BaseModel, Field
from anthropic import Anthropic

JUDGE_MODEL = "claude-sonnet-4-6"  # Pinned 4.6-generation snapshot

class Verdict(str, Enum):
    PASS = "pass"
    FAIL = "fail"
    UNKNOWN = "unknown"  # Escape hatch when evidence is missing/ambiguous

class GroundednessGrade(BaseModel):
    # Request brief evidence, not "thinking" or "reasoning" fields.
    evidence_summary: str = Field(description="1-2 sentence citation of supporting or contradicting facts.")
    verdict: Verdict

def grade_groundedness(client: Anthropic, source_context: str, candidate_answer: str) -> GroundednessGrade:
    prompt = (
        "Grade whether <answer> is fully supported by <context>. "
        "Return 'fail' if any claim contradicts or goes beyond <context>. "
        "Return 'unknown' if <context> is truncated or ambiguous.\n\n"
        f"<context>{source_context}</context>\n<answer>{candidate_answer}</answer>"
    )
    resp = client.messages.parse(
        model=JUDGE_MODEL,
        max_tokens=2048,
        thinking={"type": "adaptive", "display": "summarized"},
        output_config={"effort": "medium"},
        messages=[{"role": "user", "content": prompt}],
        output_format=GroundednessGrade,
    )
    if resp.stop_reason == "refusal":
        raise RuntimeError(f"Judge refused ({resp.stop_details})")
    parsed = resp.parsed_output
    parsed.verdict = Verdict(parsed.verdict.value.strip().lower())  # Defensive enum normalization
    return parsed

class PairwiseChoice(BaseModel):
    brief_justification: str
    winner: Literal["first", "second", "tie", "unknown"]

def pairwise_swapped(client: Anthropic, question: str, ans_a: str, ans_b: str) -> str:
    def run_once(first: str, second: str) -> str:
        r = client.messages.parse(
            model=JUDGE_MODEL,
            max_tokens=2048,
            thinking={"type": "adaptive", "display": "omitted"},
            output_config={"effort": "medium"},
            messages=[{
                "role": "user",
                "content": (
                    "Compare Response 1 and Response 2 solely on factual accuracy and conciseness. "
                    "Do not reward extra length unless factual detail differs.\n"
                    f"<question>{question}</question>\n"
                    f"<response_1>{first}</response_1>\n<response_2>{second}</response_2>"
                ),
            }],
            output_format=PairwiseChoice,
        )
        return r.parsed_output.winner.strip().lower()

    v_ab, v_ba = run_once(ans_a, ans_b), run_once(ans_b, ans_a)
    if v_ab == "unknown" or v_ba == "unknown":
        return "unknown"
    if v_ab == "first" and v_ba == "second":
        return "A"
    if v_ab == "second" and v_ba == "first":
        return "B"
    return "tie"  # Conservative swap-inconsistency -> tie (Zheng et al., 2023)

Judge drift across model versions

A judge can change underneath your metrics. From Claude 4.6 onward, a dateless model ID is canonical for that release and maps to a single fixed snapshot whose weights and configuration Anthropic does not update; updates ship under new IDs. Pre-4.6 dateless strings such as claude-sonnet-4-5 are convenience aliases that resolve to the most recent dated snapshot, whereas a 4.6-generation ID such as claude-sonnet-4-6 is not an alias — it is the snapshot. Pinning is necessary but not sufficient: weights are fixed for an ID while the serving infrastructure around it (request router, safety classifiers, sampling logic) can change over time.

Pin the whole instrument, not just the model string: snapshot ID, prompt text, output schema, thinking configuration, and effort as one versioned artifact. Pin the model ID for the platform you call and avoid moving aliases in longitudinal pipelines. Keep a frozen human-labeled anchor set and re-score it on a schedule: movement there without an application change is evaluator drift. Before promoting a new judge version, run old and new side by side on it and re-measure IRR.

Error analysis and release gates

A judge's verdict distribution is the artifact to analyze, not the headline pass rate. Do not take eval scores at face value until someone digs into the eval details and reads transcripts: if grading is unfair, tasks are ambiguous, valid solutions are penalized, or the harness constrains the model, the eval should be revised rather than the application, because the transcript is what shows whether the agent erred or the grader rejected a valid solution.

Judge confidence is not a single verdict field. Estimate it with swap consistency, repeated-sample agreement, the rate of Unknown abstentions, and calibration curves mapping confidence tiers to observed human accuracy. Route low-confidence and flipped items to human review.

Align rubric strictness with asymmetric error cost. In a safety or groundedness defect detector, where a defect is the positive class, minimize false negatives so bad outputs do not ship and accept more false-positive triage. In a quality release gate, where a pass is the positive class, a lenient judge ships regressions and an overly strict one blocks valid releases; balanced slices expose that skew immediately.

Gate in two stages. First gate the evaluator: set acceptance criteria for held-out human-judge agreement and false-pass/false-fail rates based on the cost of a wrong decision. For pairwise graders, also measure swap consistency and position effects. Treat numeric thresholds as task-specific acceptance criteria, not universal Anthropic requirements. Then use the calibrated judge in application release gates and reserve systematic human studies for judge calibration or subjective outputs where human consensus is the reference.

Decision criteria

  • Deterministic check available? Use code-based grading: fastest and most reliable, and it reserves judge calls for what needs judgment.
  • Need rates comparable across builds? Use pointwise grading with a pinned judge; a pairwise full matrix costs quadratically.
  • Need a subtle difference between two systems? Use pairwise comparison with bidirectional swapping, scoring inconsistent results as ties.
  • Task has a verifiable answer? Give the judge a trusted reference so it can compare the answer against evidence; Zheng et al. describe this method for math and reasoning grading.
  • Choosing an IRR metric? Two complete raters: kappa. Three or more raters, missing overlap, or ordinal scales: alpha.
  • Setting a gate threshold? Scale it to the cost of a wrong release decision and verify it on a balanced slice.

Before a judge gets gating authority, demand human-human IRR, judge-human IRR on a held-out slice, swap consistency, per-class precision and recall, and disagreement transcripts.

Failure modes and recovery

Common mistake Trusting an unswapped pairwise win rate

A judge that always sees the same candidate in Slot 1 reports a win rate that mostly measures slot preference. Rerun in both orders, count consistent wins only, score flips as ties or send them to human review, then recalibrate against human labels before gating.

Common mistake Putting reasoning in the judge's output schema

A literal <thinking> request or a reasoning property can trigger a reasoning_extraction refusal. The refusal response uses stop_reason: "refusal", and this refusal category has no recommended fallback model. Ask for a brief evidence_summary instead and inspect native thinking blocks with display: "summarized" when needed.

Common mistake Judging version over version with a moving alias

A pre-4.6 dateless ID such as claude-sonnet-4-5 silently retargets to a newer dated snapshot, so longitudinal score changes mix application behavior with judge changes. Pin the snapshot, re-score a frozen anchor set, and compare judges side by side before promoting one.

Lesser failures, and their recovery:

  • Raw percent agreement on an imbalanced suite. Recheck on a balanced slice and report minority-class precision and recall.
  • One blended judge for five criteria. Halo effects mask regressions; split per dimension and move rule-shaped checks to code.
  • Tuning judge prompts without transcripts. You optimize against the harness; read disagreements before editing rubrics.

Exam lens

Exam lenseditorial inference

  • Win rates jump because one model sits first, or right after a rubric change. Do not roll out or gate yet: randomize or swap presentation order, re-measure agreement against human labels, and withhold gating until the judge is calibrated.
  • A structured judge starts refusing after you add a reasoning field. That is reasoning_extraction, which has no fallback model; remove the field, use native adaptive thinking with display: "summarized" for audits, and request a short evidence_summary.
  • Raw agreement is high but kappa is low. Diagnose class imbalance and check minority-class precision and recall on balanced data; use Krippendorff's alpha rather than kappa for three or more raters, missing ratings, or 1–5 ordinal scales.
  • Eval scores drop with no application change. Suspect the evaluator: a moving pre-4.6 alias, an unpinned upgrade, or serving-infrastructure drift. Verify on the frozen anchor set first.
